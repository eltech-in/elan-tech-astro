A spreadsheet can record a request. An email can approve it. But when your team spends its day finding the latest version, chasing approvals and re-entering the same information, another standalone tool may not solve the underlying problem.

**A business portal is a controlled online workspace where customers, members, partners or employees complete agreed tasks and access the information relevant to them.** The right starting point is a workflow, not a list of dashboard features.

This guide explains what to scope, what changes the cost and which commercial questions to settle before development begins.

## Do you need a website, a portal or a CRM?

A public website explains your business and helps people enquire or buy. A portal adds authenticated tasks: submitting documents, checking progress, booking a service or accessing records. A CRM manages customer relationships and sales or service activity. These can work together, but they are not interchangeable.

For example, a prospective property buyer might enquire through a website, an agent might follow up in a CRM, and an approved partner might manage listings inside a portal. Building all three at once is not always necessary.

Before replacing existing software, ask whether configuring a suitable platform or connecting current systems would address the problem with less operational disruption. See our [custom CRM and ERP services](/services/custom-crm-erp-solutions/) for related implementation options.

## Start with one complete workflow

Write down a task from beginning to end, including what happens when something goes wrong. An illustrative service-request workflow could be:

1. A customer submits the required details.
2. A coordinator checks completeness and assigns responsibility.
3. A team member records progress and requests any missing information.
4. An authorised person approves completion.
5. The customer receives an update and the record is retained or removed under an agreed policy.

This is a planning example, not a claim about a particular client deployment. For your workflow, identify the person responsible for each step, the information they need and what counts as successful completion.

## Features to agree before requesting a quote

### User roles and access

Define who can view, create, edit, approve, export and delete each type of record. A customer should not see another customer's documents simply because both use the same portal. Administrator access should also be limited to the responsibilities that genuinely require it.

Include account invitation, password recovery, staff departures and access reviews in the brief. These are operating requirements, not just login-screen details.

### Records, forms and approvals

Specify the fields, attachments, validation rules and status changes for each record. Agree whether an approval can be withdrawn, who may correct a mistake and whether a history of changes is needed.

Avoid collecting information merely because it might be useful later. Each field creates work for the user and a responsibility for the organisation holding it.

### Notifications and reporting

Decide which updates belong inside the portal and which need email, SMS or WhatsApp delivery. Confirm provider availability, message charges, failure alerts and who follows up when a notification cannot be delivered.

For reports, name the decision each report supports. An overdue-approvals list may be more useful than a decorative dashboard with totals nobody acts on.

### Integrations and data migration

List accounting, payment, inventory, CRM and other systems the portal must exchange information with. Confirm API access, supported operations, usage charges and test accounts before promising an integration.

Agree which system is authoritative for each field, how duplicates are handled and how failed synchronisation is detected and retried. For migration, include a trial import, record reconciliation and sign-off by the people who understand the data.

## What affects business portal development cost?

There is no useful universal price without a scope. Ask suppliers to separate the following cost areas so you can compare equivalent proposals:

- **Discovery and design:** workflow mapping, role definitions, interface design and acceptance criteria.
- **Implementation:** confirmed modules, approval rules, reporting and integrations.
- **Migration and launch:** data preparation, trial imports, testing, training and deployment.
- **Operations:** hosting, backups, monitoring, message delivery and support.
- **Future changes:** upgrades, new workflows, replacement integrations and additional training.

Record taxes, third-party licences, usage limits and renewal assumptions separately. A mostly one-time development payment does not mean there are no recurring infrastructure or provider charges.

Reduce uncertainty by launching a smaller, complete workflow first. Adding more modules before the first workflow has been tested can increase both development effort and the burden on your team.

## Security, accessibility and privacy belong in the scope

Include permission testing, secure file handling, audit history where appropriate, backup restoration and incident responsibilities. Specify how production credentials are managed and who can access live records during support.

Test important journeys with a keyboard, understandable form labels, clear validation and usable mobile controls. Accessibility-first development is a process of designing, testing and correcting barriers; it is not an automatic certification or a guarantee that every deployment meets every legal requirement.

Privacy work should identify what is collected, why it is needed, which providers receive it, how long it is retained and how requests are handled. Applicable obligations depend on the organisation, its users, the data and the jurisdictions involved. Our [DPDP implementation service](/services/dpdp-compliance-implementation/) can help scope technical and content changes; legal interpretation should be reviewed with a qualified adviser.

## Agree ownership and operating responsibilities in writing

Ask these questions before accepting a proposal:

1. What application, documentation and administrator access will be delivered?
2. Are source-code access and ownership included, and subject to which payment terms?
3. Which components have separate third-party or reuse licences?
4. Can the application be deployed on your server, and what environment does it require?
5. Who controls hosting, domains, provider accounts and backups?
6. What is included in maintenance, and what is separately chargeable?
7. How will another team take over if your support arrangement changes?

At eLan Technology, source-code rights, application ownership, licensing, delivery and support depend on the signed proposal and payment terms. Client-server deployment can be scoped, subject to technical suitability. Hosting, maintenance, third-party services and upgrades are separately scoped and charged unless expressly included.

Do not assume a fixed delivery window from a product demo. A relatively unchanged configuration and a portal with substantial new functionality require different estimates. Confirm the schedule after requirements, dependencies and client approvals are agreed.

## Test the workflow before accepting the portal

Ask for acceptance tests covering normal use and exceptions: an incomplete request, a rejected approval, an unauthorised record, an unavailable integration and a failed notification. Confirm how the team restores a backup and operates the system if a provider is temporarily unavailable.

Use representative test data rather than unnecessary live personal information. Agree who signs off each workflow and how unresolved issues affect launch. Training and an operating guide should explain what your team does after the developers leave.
