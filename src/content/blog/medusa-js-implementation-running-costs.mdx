A Medusa.js proposal can look inexpensive if it prices the storefront and leaves out what happens after launch. Before comparing quotes, ask who will run the store, maintain its integrations and recover it if something goes wrong.

<div class="tldr" data-speakable>
**Budget separately for implementation, monthly operations, usage charges and future changes. Compare the same scope over 12 and 36 months. Confirm who owns accounts, who responds to incidents and what the signed proposal actually includes.**
</div>

## 1. Implementation: price workflows, not just pages

A catalogue, a cart and an admin dashboard do not define the whole project. List what a customer and a store operator must be able to do. Include exceptions such as unavailable items, rejected payments, cancellations, refunds and stock discrepancies.

- **Storefront:** catalogue structure, variants, search, mobile checkout, accessibility and content management.
- **Commerce rules:** delivery eligibility, pricing, promotions, repeat orders and any subscription logic.
- **Integrations:** payment, shipping, inventory, accounting and customer communications. Confirm provider access and test environments before committing.
- **Migration:** data cleaning, field mapping, trial import, reconciliation, URL redirects and a cutover plan.
- **Launch:** acceptance tests, operator training, deployment notes and the initial support window.

The strongest cost control is often a smaller first release. For example, launch one delivery area and a limited catalogue before adding complex fulfilment rules. Keep the later work visible in the proposal rather than silently assuming it is included.

## 2. Hosting: compare responsibilities as well as invoices

[Medusa’s deployment documentation](https://docs.medusajs.com/learn/deployment) describes separate storefront and application deployments, and explains the database and supporting services involved. Ask for a proposed architecture specific to your store, not a generic server recommendation.

For each environment, identify what is included: storefront, application, database, background jobs, storage, backups, logs, alerts and traffic allowances. A staging environment may be important for testing upgrades and payment changes without disturbing live orders.

Managed infrastructure can reduce the work of provisioning and operating components, but ask who is responsible for your custom code, integration failures and checkout regressions. With self-hosting, make those responsibilities explicit instead of assuming a lower hosting invoice means a lower total cost.

## 3. Ongoing costs: separate predictable and variable charges

**Predictable costs** may include infrastructure, monitoring, maintenance and a support agreement. Specify what that agreement covers: security updates, backups, restore drills, incident response, business-hours coverage and routine operator assistance.

**Variable costs** may include payment processing, message delivery, shipping-provider usage, storage and traffic. Model low, expected and peak volumes. If fees depend on both order count and order value, estimate both rather than using traffic alone.

**Occasional costs** include major upgrades, replacement integrations, catalogue restructuring and feature work. A maintenance agreement does not necessarily include a new integration or a substantial upgrade. Ask how each is estimated and approved.

## 4. Build a 12- and 36-month comparison

Use the same assumptions for every proposal:

1. Start with implementation, migration, launch and training.
2. Add the recurring charges for the comparison period.
3. Add usage-based charges at the same order volumes.
4. Allow for planned upgrades and separately quoted changes.
5. Record tax, currency and renewal assumptions separately.

For example, in a **fictional budgeting exercise**, a build of 100 units, fixed operations of 5 units per month, usage of 2 units per month and 20 units for planned changes totals 204 units over 12 months. This is arithmetic, not a price or benchmark. Replace every input with your own written quotes and avoid counting costs that are already bundled.

If you are choosing between a managed storefront and a custom commerce stack, read our [Shopify vs Medusa.js comparison](/blog/ecommerce/shopify-vs-medusa-js-2026/) first. Then ask each supplier to price the same customer journeys and support expectations.

## 5. Confirm what handover actually means

Ask who controls the repository, domain, hosting account, backups, payment account and vendor credentials. Agree what documentation and administrator access will be delivered. If a different team takes over, it should be clear how to deploy, restore data and trace a failed order.

Source-code access, ownership, reuse rights, licences, deployment and maintenance are governed by the signed proposal and payment terms. They are not automatic consequences of choosing Medusa.js.

## What to send for a useful quote

Share your product structure, sales channels, expected volumes, existing systems, must-have workflows and who will operate the store. Include a representative order journey and two or three exceptions the team handles today. A good proposal will distinguish confirmed work, assumptions and options that still need discovery.
