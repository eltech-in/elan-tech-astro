We had already been building websites for three years before we gave the work a name.

From 2002, it was freelance projects and a small startup unit in Nagpur: one machine, work that came through word of mouth and invoices written by hand. On 15 August 2005, it officially became eLan Technology.

We did not choose the date for symbolism. It was simply the day everything was ready. Twenty-one years later, sharing a birthday with Independence Day has come to mean something we did not plan for. The question we spend much of our working life answering is ultimately a question about freedom:

**Who actually owns your business online?**

Most Indian business owners assume the answer is obvious. It often is not.

**In brief:** Digital independence means owning your domain, controlling your website and data, receiving all project access, and understanding the true cost of changing platforms or providers. The right technology may be hosted or open source. What matters is making that choice with clear information.

## Digital independence is easy to celebrate and easy to lose

In 1947, independence was a moment everyone could point to. A flag went up, a speech was given and the change was visible.

Digital dependence rarely arrives with a warning. A business may discover one day that the website it thought it owned is still controlled by a former freelancer, or that moving away from a platform will cost far more than expected.

We see three versions of this regularly among businesses in [Nagpur](/web-design-company-nagpur/), Bhopal, Raipur, Mumbai and beyond.

### The domain that was never yours

A business contacts us for a website redesign. We ask for domain access. There is a long pause.

The domain was registered by a freelancer years ago using the freelancer's name and email address. That person has changed their number, moved away or stopped responding. The business has spent years building recognition on a web address it does not legally control.

This is one of the most common forms of digital dependence we encounter, and one of the easiest to prevent. Your organisation should control the registrar account, recovery email and billing details for its domain.

### The platform that raised the rent

A growing direct-to-consumer brand builds on a software-as-a-service commerce platform. Sales increase, but so do the platform costs. A useful feature moves to a higher plan. Transaction charges apply to every order. Leaving now means rebuilding the store and migrating its data.

Hosted commerce is still the right choice for many businesses, and we happily build on it. The problem is not the platform. The problem is committing without understanding the long-term cost, data access and exit route.

### The website nobody can open

A company inherits a website from an agency that no longer responds. There is no documentation, repository access or record of the plugins and services used. The hosting account belongs to someone else.

The website works until it does not. When something breaks, nobody has the access or knowledge needed to repair it.

Knowledge can create lock-in just as easily as a domain name. A proper project handover matters.

## What 21 years taught us about website ownership

We did not begin with a written philosophy. It developed after seeing the same problems often enough that fixing them became non-negotiable.

Every domain we register goes in the client's name. The client is the legal owner and can transfer it without paying us a transfer charge.

Every completed project ships with its keys. This includes the relevant repository access, hosting credentials, documentation and administrator logins. These are handed over at launch, not held back as insurance against a client leaving.

We also explain the exit cost before recommending the entry route. A hosted platform may offer speed, convenience and easier daily management. Open infrastructure may offer greater control, but it comes with responsibility for maintenance. Neither answer is right for every business.

Our [business products and portals](/products/) grew from the same principle. Clients needed conference, society, real estate and hospitality systems they could use and control without stitching together a long list of subscriptions.

## Shopify and Medusa.js: two valid paths to ecommerce independence

Twenty-one years produces a lot of work. In 2026, two ecommerce projects helped us explain digital independence more clearly because they sit on opposite sides of the platform decision.

### Rangresha: choosing Shopify for speed and simplicity

[Rangresha](https://rangresha.com/) is a handcrafted products store built around the work of women artisans from NGO Aroha in Nagpur. We created its storefront through our [custom Shopify design and development service](/services/ecommerce/shopify-development/).

Shopify was the right choice. The team needed to launch quickly, manage products without technical help and focus on its craft rather than infrastructure. A hosted platform provided the operating simplicity the business needed. The domain and brand assets remain in the client's control.

### Puritas: choosing Medusa.js for complete control

[Puritas](https://puritas.in/), a cold-pressed oils and wellness brand from Butibori, needed subscription billing, wholesale pricing and Indian payment methods that required deeper customisation.

We built its store with [Medusa.js ecommerce development](/services/ecommerce/medusa-js-development/) and created the required subscription, Razorpay, PhonePe and shipping modules. The project required more custom development upfront. In return, Puritas owns the commerce stack and can adapt its workflows without waiting for a platform roadmap.

Two businesses chose two different routes. Both decisions were correct because they were based on what each business genuinely needed.

That is the honest version of digital independence. Maximum ownership is not automatically the best option. The goal is to understand the trade-offs and make the decision yourself.

## Twenty-four years of work and twenty-one under one name

The technology has changed completely since 2002. We began with static HTML and table-based layouts, worked through multiple generations of content management systems and watched mobile commerce become part of everyday life. Today, we build with modern web frameworks, custom commerce platforms, accessibility standards and AI-assisted development.

Three things have not changed.

### Clients still want useful results

Most clients want the same outcome they wanted in 2005: a website that loads quickly, works on the device in their hand and helps bring in business. The technology around that goal has been rebuilt many times. The goal itself has barely moved.

### Speed still beats unnecessary complexity

An elaborate website nobody waits for is worth less than a clear website that appears immediately. Performance remains part of the user experience, not a technical score added at the end.

### You cannot outsource caring

Tools have become extraordinary, and AI now helps us complete work that once took much longer. It still cannot decide what a business truly needs, notice that a brief is solving the wrong problem or choose the honest recommendation over the more profitable one. That responsibility remains human.
